Nearly every oyster injury is one of two things: **the knife skidding off a shell
into the hand holding it**, or **the shell shattering because someone forced the
blade into the wrong place.** Both come from the same mistake — trying to open
an oyster by pushing harder.

An oyster does not open by force. It opens by finding one small hinge and
twisting. Once you have felt it, the knife barely moves.

## What you need

- **A real oyster knife.** Short, stiff, blunt-tipped, with a guard where the
  blade meets the handle. The blunt tip is the safety feature: it will not
  penetrate a shell _or_ a palm. A paring knife is the single worst choice here —
  it is sharp, thin, and flexes, so it skids, then snaps.
- **A folded kitchen towel.** This does the actual safety work. See below.
- **A tray or plate with a lip,** because the liquor is the best part and you
  will otherwise pour it on the counter.

A chainmail glove is excellent if you have one. A folded towel is most of the
benefit for none of the money, and it also grips the shell better.

## Before you open anything: check it

The FDA's test is the simplest one there is — **"live clams, oysters, and mussels
will close when the shell is tapped. If they don't close when tapped, do not
select them."** Tap the shell. A live oyster clamps shut, sometimes slowly. One
that stays open is not asleep.

Throw away any with **cracked or broken shells** (FDA), and the CDC's
instruction for shellfish you are about to cook is to **"discard any shellfish
with open shells before cooking."**

One oyster that smells wrong when you open it — sharp, sulphurous, nothing like
the sea — goes in the bin, and so does its liquor. Do not taste it to check. But
note what the CDC says, which is the harder truth: **"it is impossible to tell
that an oyster is bad by looking at it."** Smell and appearance catch a _dead_
oyster. They do not catch a contaminated one. That is a different problem, and
it is covered in [raw oysters and Vibrio](/guides/raw-oysters-and-vibrio/).

## The towel grip

This is the part people skip, and it is the part that prevents the injury.

Fold a kitchen towel into a thick pad. Put the oyster on it, **cup side down,
flat side up** — the cup is the deeper, rounder shell, and keeping it underneath
means the liquor stays in. Now fold the towel back over the oyster so your
holding hand is **on top of the towel, not on the shell**, with only the hinge
end sticking out.

Your hand is now behind a layer of cloth and behind the oyster. If the knife
skids, it skids into a towel.

Never shuck an oyster held in your bare palm. That is the classic hand-wound
geometry: a convex shell, a blade pointed at your hand, and your own weight
behind it.

## Finding the hinge

Look at the oyster end-on. One end is a rough point where the two shells are
joined — that is the **hinge**. The other end is a wider lip. Everything happens
at the hinge.

The hinge is not a seam you cut. It is a ligament, and there is a small gap or
notch just to one side of the point where the knife tip will seat. Set the tip
there and feel for the moment it stops sliding — a millimetre or two of purchase
is all you need.

## The twist

**Work the knife in a few millimetres, then rotate it like a key.**

Not a lever. Not a shove. A quarter-turn of the handle while the tip stays put.
The hinge ligament pops — you will hear it — and the top shell lifts slightly.
That is the whole trick, and if you are pushing hard enough to worry about your
hand, you are in the wrong spot. Pull the knife out, look at the hinge again, and
reseat it.

Then:

1. **Slide the blade along the inside of the flat top shell**, keeping it pressed
   up against that shell, to cut the adductor muscle holding it down. Staying
   against the top shell is what stops you shredding the oyster.
2. **Lift the top shell away** and discard it.
3. **Run the knife under the oyster** to free it from the bottom shell, so it
   lifts cleanly when someone tips it into their mouth. Leave it sitting in its
   cup.
4. **Check the liquor.** Pick out any shell fragments. Do not rinse the oyster —
   you would be pouring away the flavour, and the liquor is not dirty.

## If it will not open

Put it down and pick up another one. A stubborn hinge means your tip is in the
wrong place, and the next thing that happens if you force it is the shell
breaking and the knife continuing on its original path.

Some oysters are genuinely harder — deeper cups, thicker hinges, older animals.
Rotating the oyster and approaching the hinge from slightly the other side
usually finds the gap.

## If you do cut yourself

Stop shucking, wash it properly with soap and water, and take it seriously rather
than wrapping a paper towel round it and carrying on. A shellfish-handling wound
is not an ordinary kitchen cut: _Vibrio_ can infect a wound as well as a gut, and
the CDC's handling advice includes washing hands with soap and water after
handling raw shellfish and wearing protective gloves if you are at increased
risk of infection. If a cut from a shell or a knife becomes red, swollen, or
painful out of proportion to its size, get it looked at and mention the
shellfish.

## Then

Cold plate, no ice-melt pooling in the shells, and nothing on them until you have
tried the first one plain — it is the only way to taste where it grew. The
