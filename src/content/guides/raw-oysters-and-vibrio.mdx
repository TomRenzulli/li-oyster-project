This is the one page on this site where we are not going to be charming about
oysters.

Long Island grows very good ones, and the overwhelming majority of people eat
them raw with no consequence at all. But raw shellfish carries a real risk that
falls unevenly: for most people it is a small one, and for a specific group of
people it is severe. The difference between those two groups is not luck or
tolerance. It is a short, knowable list of medical conditions.

So: what the public-health agencies actually say, quoted, with the gaps marked.

## What Vibrio is

_Vibrio_ is a genus of bacteria that lives naturally in coastal salt water. It is
not a sign of pollution and it is not a sign of a badly run farm — it is part of
the environment the oyster filters for a living. Oysters concentrate what is in
the water, which is the same reason they taste like where they grew.

The CDC's position on who can be affected is blunt: **"Anyone can get a _Vibrio_
infection."**

## Who is at higher risk

The CDC lists these as factors that increase the risk of a serious infection:

- Having **liver disease**, cancer, diabetes, HIV, or thalassemia
- Receiving **immune-suppressing therapy** for the treatment of disease
- Taking **medicine to decrease stomach acid levels**
- Having had **recent stomach surgery**

Two of those surprise people. **Liver disease is the big one** — the CDC's
prevention page singles it out, saying its precautions are "especially important
if you have an underlying medical condition, especially liver disease." And
**routine acid-reducing medication counts.** Stomach acid is part of your
defence; a daily proton-pump inhibitor for ordinary heartburn is on the CDC's
list alongside chemotherapy. If you take one and did not know that, this page
has done its job.

The FDA frames its susceptible groups slightly differently, listing **pregnant
women, children, older adults, and "persons with weakened immune systems (such
as transplant patients and individuals with HIV/AIDS, cancer, and diabetes)."**
Note that the FDA's list does _not_ mention liver disease and the CDC's does not
mention pregnancy or age. They are not contradicting each other; they are two
agencies writing for different purposes. **Read both lists as one list.**

## What the agencies recommend

For people in those groups, the FDA is unambiguous. Susceptible groups should
avoid **"raw oysters, even if they are treated after they have been harvested."**

That clause matters, because post-harvest processing is often sold as making
oysters safe. The FDA's own wording: **"Post-harvest treatment eliminates some
naturally occurring pathogens, but does not remove all pathogens that can cause
illness."** Treated is not the same as safe.

And the CDC's general prevention advice — written for everyone, not just
high-risk groups — is: **"Do not eat raw or undercooked oysters or other seafood.
Cook them before eating."**

We are a site about oyster farms, so let us be straight about the tension there:
that is stricter than how most people, including most of the people who grow
them, actually eat oysters. We are not going to pretend the CDC says something
softer than it does. What we will say is that the decision is yours to make with
accurate information, and that if you are on the list above, this is not a close
call.

## Symptoms

The CDC lists: **watery diarrhea, stomach cramps, nausea, vomiting, fever,
chills.**

Most _Vibrio_ infections from oysters, per the CDC, "result in only diarrhea and
vomiting." Some — _Vibrio vulnificus_ in particular — "can be severe."

If you are in a higher-risk group and you develop these symptoms after eating raw
shellfish, say _that sentence_ to a clinician: that you ate raw shellfish, when,
and what your risk factors are. It changes what they look for.

We could not find a Vibrio-specific figure for how long symptoms take to appear,
so we are not giving you one.

## Three things that are not true

**"You can tell a bad oyster by looking at it."** You cannot. The CDC:
**"It is impossible to tell that an oyster is bad by looking at it. An oyster
that contains harmful germs doesn't look, smell, or even taste different from any
other oyster."**

**"Hot sauce, lemon, or a drink kills it."** It does not. The CDC, specifically:
**"Hot sauce and lemon juice don't kill _Vibrio_. Drinking alcohol while eating
oysters doesn't kill _Vibrio_ either."**

**"Only eat oysters in months with an R in them."** The R-month rule predates
refrigeration and modern sanitation, and it is not a safety standard. The CDC's
framing is that while most _Vibrio_ infections occur during warmer months,
**"infections have been reported year-round"** and **"you can get sick from
eating oysters during any month of the year."** Seasonality is real — the CDC
says _Vibrio_ are **"found in higher numbers in May through October, when water
temperatures are warmer"** — but "higher numbers in summer" is a risk gradient,
not a safe season. There is no month in which the list of at-risk people gets
shorter.

## If you want to eat them cooked

The CDC gives times rather than an internal temperature for oysters. In the
shell: **"Boil until the shells open and continue boiling for another 3–5
minutes,"** or add to an already-steaming steamer and **"cook for another 4–9
minutes."** Shucked: boil for at least 3 minutes; fry in oil at 375&nbsp;°F for at
least 3 minutes; broil 3 inches from heat for 3 minutes; or bake at 450&nbsp;°F for
10 minutes.

On discarding: the CDC says to **"discard any shellfish with open shells before
cooking"** and to **"only eat shellfish that open during cooking — throw out any
that don't open fully during cooking."**

## Buying and keeping them

The FDA's selection advice for live shellfish in the shell: look for the tags on
the sack or container, and use the tap test — **"live clams, oysters, and mussels
will close when the shell is tapped. If they don't close when tapped, do not
select them."** Discard any with **cracked or broken shells**.

For storage, the FDA's general seafood guidance is a refrigerator at **40&nbsp;°F or
below** if you will use it within two days, checked with an actual refrigerator
thermometer.

A lot of oyster-storage folklore — cup-side down, under a damp cloth, never in a
sealed bag, never in fresh water — is probably sound and is certainly widespread,
but we could not source it to a government or extension publication, so it is not
stated as fact here. It is on our list to verify properly.

## Where this leaves you

If you have liver disease, a suppressed immune system, cancer, diabetes, HIV,
thalassemia, recent stomach surgery, or you take acid-reducing medication: the
agencies agree, and the answer is cooked, not raw, in every month of the year.

If you are not on that list: you are making an ordinary informed-risk decision,
the kind you make about a lot of food. Buy from somewhere that keeps its tags,
keep them cold, and eat them fresh. The farms in [our directory](/farms/) are
real, licensed operations whose facts we checked and dated.
